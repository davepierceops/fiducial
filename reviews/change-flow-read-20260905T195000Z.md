## Skepticism

**Where does "one read" hide a review that will not happen?** Three places, in descending order of what it costs.

The skeptic pass is the first. F-2 is the finding, but the risk is worth stating separately from the rule violation: the corpus's reason for two passes is that the same reader who has just satisfied itself a change is good is the worst reader to ask where it is lying. Making skepticism the third section of a single read does not merely violate R0492 — it puts the two questions in one session in an order where the second is answered by a party already committed to the first. The document's own defence, that the split matters for consequential deltas, concedes the mechanism and then applies it to the minority of changes.

The code-quality review is the second. Line 65 makes "every pull request gets a code review by an agent" the strongest single obligation in the document, and lines 66–68 immediately hand its content to a standard that is not named and retire the only document that currently supplies it (F-11). An obligation with no rules behind it is satisfied by any agent that returns a review artifact saying nothing — which is exactly what the retiring document's ban on empty reviews exists to prevent. The interval between retirement and adoption is where the review does not happen.

The trivial exemption is the third (F-4). It is small by construction and self-classified by the party it benefits, which is the combination that does not stay small.

**Where does "charged once per delta" let a large change through with less review?** At the top end, and the document does not see it. The old flow charged per meaningful change: stages 9 and 10, two roles, every time. The new flow charges per delta, and a delta has no upper bound — line 23 says so explicitly. So a delta containing forty meaningful changes gets one read where the old flow gave forty pairs of reads. That is the intended saving and it is defensible for a spec branch, where the diff is prose and one reader genuinely can hold it. It is not obviously defensible for code, and the document applies the same rule to both. The one guard that would help — the delta closes when it exceeds what one reviewer can read whole — is stated in line 17's definition and withdrawn in line 23's example, so it guards nothing. F-8 carries the fix.

**Is "two readers for the consequential class" enough, and is the class reachable?** Reachable, yes, but not from this document. R0535 makes the class exhaustive and R0536 supplies the tie-breaker — when unsure, treat it as consequential and ask — and a session that opens the policy can answer the question in a minute. A session holding only change-flow.md gets a row range and no characterisation (line 82), so it cannot self-classify at all; it must fetch fourteen rows to learn whether it needs one reader or two. That is a small friction with a large failure mode, because the cheap answer under uncertainty is "one reader" and the corpus's default is the opposite.

Enough, no. The class was built to decide who authorises exposure to users (R0534, R0535), and it maps poorly onto how hard a change is to review. The two failure directions are not symmetric: a schema migration of four lines gets two readers, which costs a session; an unbounded internal refactor gets one, which costs the review. Only the second is expensive, and it is the one the class does not catch. This is the document's largest open risk and it is not among the three questions the closing section reserves for Dave — it should be.
